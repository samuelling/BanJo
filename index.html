How the sound is made

A drum with a neck. A guitar's strings drive a wooden plate; a banjo's drive a drumhead, a sheet of film about 0.18 mm thick and a few grams heavy, stretched like a trampoline. That one difference is most of the banjo's voice. The head is so light that the bridge moves about ten times more easily than a guitar's, so string energy pours out fast: loud, bright attacks and short notes.

The head. Its vibration patterns are the classic drum modes, rings and spokes from Bessel functions, raised by tension and lowered by the air that has to move with the head. Calibrated so a bare 11-inch head over an open pot rings its lowest mode at 290 Hz, as measured on a real banjo (Woodhouse, Politzer & Mansour). The tension slider reads like a DrumDial; tap the head to hear where you've set it.

The bridge. A maple bar on three feet, standing on the head. It bounces, rocks end to end, bends and sways, and the strings press on it at their own places: the 1st and 5th strings rock it most, the 3rd not at all, so every string hears a different bridge. Its mass sitting on the springs of the strings and the stretched head makes the banjo's main formant, around 500–800 Hz; the rocking legs add a bump near 3 kHz and the bridge's bending another near 5 kHz. Lighter bridge: brighter. Steeper tailpiece angle: more down-force, stiffer springs, a higher formant.

Behind the head. Open back: the air in the pot is a short pipe closed by the head; it pumps out of the back, and a belly against it muffles it. Resonator: a closed box whose air leaves through the gap around the flange, splitting the head's lowest mode in two and throwing the sound forward.

Tone ring and rim. A heavy bell-bronze ring holds the head's edge firm, so less energy leaks into the wood (longer, brighter notes) and adds a faint metallic ring of its own. A wood rim loses more.

Strings. Five steel strings, the short 5th starting at its peg at the 5th fret. Each is a sum of its own modes with measured losses; how fast each harmonic drains depends on the bridge's motion at that exact frequency. The strings' tension wobbles as they vibrate, and over the break angle that pushes down on the bridge too (Politzer's string-stretching effect).

Picks. Metal fingerpicks and a plastic thumbpick are small cantilevers that the string slides off; the fingers pull up, the thumb pushes down. In clawhammer the back of the nail falls down through the string toward the head, part pluck, part strike, and the thumb catches the 5th string.

The hands. Rolls are the classic Scruggs cells of eight notes; clawhammer is bum-ditty, double thumbing and drop thumb; backup chops on 2 and 4. Timing drifts and scatters like a real player's, fingers can't repeat faster than they recover, and the thumb leads the beat.

What's estimated. Head-tension mapping, tone-ring modes, the bridge's internal stiffness and the right-hand timing numbers are physics-based estimates, not banjo measurements. Record a take and compare it with a real banjo using the repo's analysis script to tune them.
